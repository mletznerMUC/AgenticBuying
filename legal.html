Agentic buying through a legal lens

How AI agents plan, negotiate, buy and report advertising on behalf of companies, step by step, mapped to the two current protocol families (AdCP, the Ad Context Protocol, and AAMP, IAB Tech Lab's Agentic Advertising Management Protocols), and where liability sits at each step. Written for in-house counsel, contract managers and compliance teams who need to understand the process before they can allocate its risks.

Updated 1 October 2026 · all claims on this page verified on that date

speculative This page is not legal advice. The protocol descriptions are verified against the specifications. The legal readings in each "Legal lens" block and in the liability map are an interpretation from an EU and German law perspective. Neither protocol allocates liability, so those readings apply general law to a new process. The actual allocation in any deal depends on the contracts in place. Both protocol families are evolving quickly, so check the current versions before relying on a specific field or task name.

Four principles to start from

  1. Agents are not legal persons. Every declaration an agent sends is attributed to the company that deployed it.
  2. Protocols define messages, not liability. AdCP and AAMP standardize data exchange. Who pays when something goes wrong is decided by contract and statute.
  3. Human checkpoints exist, so use them. Both families allow human approval steps. Contracts should say which ones are mandatory.
  4. Logs are your evidence. Signed messages and audit logs are what the parties will rely on in a dispute.

The process at a glance

Nine parties take part in an agentic buy. Three are companies, where people make the decisions: the advertiser, its agency and the publisher. Six are software: the buyer and sales agents that do the negotiating, and four services they call. The figure shows where each sits. Each of the nine steps below lists the messages exchanged between these parties. Step 1 — Mandate and set-up

The question: who is the agent acting for, and within which limits? · Human in the loop: a person sets the limits · Legal exposure: high

Before anything is bought, a person decides what the agent may do: total budget, campaign goals, brand rules, which sellers are allowed and the maximum value of a single order. The buyer agent is configured with these limits. It then opens a commercial account with each seller, which records who the brand is, who operates the agent, and who receives the invoice. In legal terms this is a power of attorney written in machine-readable form, plus opening a customer account with a supplier.

Agency → buyer agentsoftwaresync_plans, brand.jsonBuyer agent configuration
Buyer agent → sales agentsoftwaresync_accountsOpenDirect account set-up

AdCP

  • The accounts protocol models four entities: brand, account, operator and agent. Tasks: sync_accounts, list_accounts.
  • Two billing models: the operator (or a brand buying direct) is invoiced, or the agent consolidates billing.
  • brand.json publishes the company's identity, agent endpoints and signing keys.
  • sync_plans registers the campaign plan for later governance checks.

AAMP

  • The buyer agent is an open-source reference implementation in AAMP's agentic protocols pillar.
  • Account set-up uses OpenDirect, the existing IAB Tech Lab standard for guaranteed buys that AAMP builds on.
  • speculative AAMP publishes no dedicated "mandate" object. Authority limits stay in the buyer's own systems and contracts.

Legal lens

High exposure — attribution of agent declarations

An AI agent has no legal capacity. Under the prevailing German and EU view, orders it generates are attributed to the company that deploys it, as "computer declarations". The agency rules of §§ 164 ff. BGB apply at most by analogy. The principal can be bound even by unexpected outputs.

Risk sits with: the advertiser, and the agency if it contracts in its own name.
Mitigation: a written mandate defining scope; hard caps per order, per day and in total; an explicit clause that the agent's output binds only within its configured limits.

High exposure — who is the contracting party and debtor?

The billing model decides who owes payment: operator-billed or agent-consolidated. That affects credit risk, the agency's role (acting as agent or trading as principal) and its transparency duties toward the advertiser.

Risk sits with: the agency as operator, and the advertiser.
Mitigation: fix the billing model and the contracting entity in the framework agreement and mirror it in the account set-up.

Medium exposure — liability of the agent's provider

If the agent software malfunctions, recourse against its vendor depends on the license or SaaS terms, which often cap liability. The revised Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 treats software as a product; member states must transpose it by 9 December 2026.

Risk sits with: the agent technology provider, to the extent agreed.
Mitigation: service levels, a warranty that configured limits are enforced, carve-outs from the liability cap for orders outside the mandate, insurance.

Questions to ask the business

  • Who in our organization may change the agent's limits, and is that logged?
  • What is the maximum value of a single agent-placed order?
  • Which billing model applies, and who is the debtor?
  • Does the agent vendor contract cover losses from orders outside configured limits?

Legal sources: § 164 BGB; Directive (EU) 2024/2853 · last verified 2026-10-01

Step 2 — Discovery and identity check

The question: is the counterparty who it claims to be? · Human in the loop: none, automated · Legal exposure: medium

The buyer agent looks for suitable sellers and checks that the sales agent is actually authorized to sell that publisher's inventory. It consults a registry and a file the publisher publishes on its own website listing its authorized agents. This stops spoofed sellers and unauthorized resellers from taking the money.

Legal lens

Medium exposure — impersonation and unauthorized resale

A buyer that pays an agent the publisher never authorized may have paid the wrong party. Recovery is then a fraud or unjust-enrichment claim against a party that may be unreachable.

Risk sits with: the buyer, unless the contract shifts the verification duty.
Mitigation: require verification against adagents.json or the registry and signed requests; a warranty of authorization from the seller.

Medium exposure — relying on a registry

A registry listing is a technical identity statement, not a certificate of legal compliance or solvency. Check the registry's terms; it will usually disclaim liability.

Risk sits with: the party relying on the listing.
Mitigation: treat the registry as one input; keep your own know-your-customer, sanctions and vendor checks.

Lower exposure — the publisher's duty to keep its files current

Outdated authorization files can let former partners keep selling. The publisher controls the file, so it carries the risk of keeping it current.

Risk sits with: the publisher.
Mitigation: an internal process that updates the authorization files when a sales contract ends.

Questions to ask the business

  • Do we require cryptographically signed requests from counterparties?
  • Who verifies the counterparty, and is the check logged?
  • Does the seller contractually warrant that its agent is authorized?

Step 3 — Brief and proposal

The question: from a request for proposals to a priced offer — are the parties negotiating yet? · Human in the loop: optional, on the seller side · Legal exposure: medium

The buyer agent sends a brief, in natural language plus structured fields: target group, budget, dates, goals. The sales agent replies with matching products, prices and delivery forecasts, sometimes as a complete proposal. A person on the seller side may review the reply, especially for large or custom deals. This is the agentic version of the request-for-proposals exchange that used to run on email and spreadsheets.

Legal lens

Medium exposure — binding offer or invitation to treat?

Is a proposal a binding offer the buyer agent can accept, or only an invitation to make an offer? Neither protocol says. Forecasts are estimates, not promises.

Risk sits with: both sides, as uncertainty.
Mitigation: terms stating that proposals are non-binding until a defined message confirms them (see step 6), and that forecasts are not guaranteed.

Medium exposure — confidential information in briefs

Briefs carry budgets and strategy and pass through third-party agents and possibly language-model providers. Trade-secret protection under Directive (EU) 2016/943 and the German GeschGehG requires "appropriate secrecy measures".

Risk sits with: the advertiser and agency.
Mitigation: confidentiality agreements with agent vendors; no-training and data-retention clauses; sending only the fields a seller needs.

Medium exposure — algorithmic price coordination

Agents exchanging structured pricing data at scale could facilitate collusion under Art. 101 TFEU (Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union), even without human intent.

Risk sits with: sellers and buyers deploying agents.
Mitigation: a competition-law review of which pricing data is shared; no competitor-sensitive data through shared vendors.

Lower exposure — misleading product descriptions

Inaccurate statements about reach or audience quality can trigger pre-contractual liability (culpa in contrahendo, § 311(2) BGB).

Risk sits with: the seller.
Mitigation: accuracy obligations for structured product data and an agreed source of truth.

Questions to ask the business

  • Which message turns a proposal into a binding contract?
  • Can briefs contain personal data or trade secrets, and who may see them?
  • Do agent vendors retain or train on our brief data?

Legal sources: Directive (EU) 2016/943; GeschGehG; Art. 101 TFEU; § 311 BGB · last verified 2026-10-01

Legal lens

High exposure — lawful basis and consent

Audience segments are usually personal data. Access to a user's device needs consent under Art. 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive, in Germany § 25 TDDDG; profiling needs a lawful basis under Art. 6 GDPR. The Court of Justice of the EU held in C-604/22 (IAB Europe) that a consent string of the Transparency and Consent Framework can be personal data.

Risk sits with: the advertiser, data provider and publisher, depending on their roles.
Mitigation: consent signals travel with the data; warranties of lawful collection; audit rights.

High exposure — controller roles

Several parties deciding on purposes and means can be joint controllers (Art. 26 GDPR; Court of Justice, C-40/17 Fashion ID). An agent that chooses segments by itself does not remove the deploying company's role as controller.

Risk sits with: the advertiser, data provider and platform together.
Mitigation: joint-controller or processor agreements (Art. 26 or 28 GDPR) that cover activations an agent initiates.

High exposure — prohibited targeting

On online platforms, the Digital Services Act bans ads based on profiling with special-category data (Art. 26(3)) and profiling-based ads to minors (Art. 28(2)). Inferred interests can reveal health, religion or sexuality.

Risk sits with: the platform and the advertiser.
Mitigation: block lists of sensitive segments in the agent's configuration; the governance check in step 5.

Questions to ask the business

  • Is each segment backed by documented consent or another lawful basis?
  • Have we mapped controller and processor roles for data an agent activates?
  • Are sensitive and minor-related segments technically excluded?

Legal sources: GDPR (EU) 2016/679; ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC; § 25 TDDDG; Digital Services Act (EU) 2022/2065; C-604/22; C-40/17 · last verified 2026-10-01

Step 5 — Governance and compliance check

The question: does the plan comply with policy and law before money is committed? · Human in the loop: escalation to a person · Legal exposure: high

Before money is committed, a governance agent checks the plan: budget authority, brand safety, allowed websites and sector rules. If a rule is triggered or a limit exceeded, the decision is escalated to a person, whose approval is recorded and travels with the transaction.

AdCP

  • The governance protocol, new in 3.0, covers property lists, content standards and campaign governance: sync_plans, check_governance, report_plan_outcome, get_plan_audit_logs.

  • IAB Tech Lab's published AAMP components include no dedicated governance or compliance protocol. The Agent Registry adds disclosure.
  • speculative In practice, policy enforcement stays with the platforms and the contracts between the parties.

Legal lens

High exposure — automation is no defense

Sector rules apply in full: pharmaceutical advertising (HWG), gambling (the German state treaty GlüStV), financial promotions, and the anti-discrimination rules for job and housing ads (AGG). Liability stays with the advertiser and, depending on the rule, the publisher.

Risk sits with: the advertiser first, the publisher second.
Mitigation: make enforce mode and human review mandatory for regulated categories, by contract and by internal policy.

High exposure — advisory or enforce matters in court

If governance only advised and the agent went ahead anyway, it will be hard to argue that adequate organizational measures were in place.

Risk sits with: the operator and the advertiser.
Mitigation: document which mode is used and why; review escalations regularly.

Medium exposure — audit logs as evidence

A signed record of who approved what, and when, is strong evidence of compliance and of the scope of authority.

Risk sits with: all parties.
Mitigation: retention periods and access rights for audit logs in the contract.

Questions to ask the business

  • Which ad categories always require human approval?
  • Does governance run in enforce mode for those?
  • Who on the buyer side may approve escalations?

Legal sources: HWG; AGG · last verified 2026-10-01

Step 6 — Commitment: the media buy

The question: when is a binding contract formed? · Human in the loop: required for guaranteed deals · Legal exposure: high

The buyer agent places the order: products, budget, flight dates, purchase order number. Small or standard orders may be confirmed at once. Larger or guaranteed deals go to a person on the seller side for approval, like signing an insertion order. The buyer receives a confirmation ID and the metrics the seller commits to report. For lawyers this is the key step: offer, acceptance and terms.

MessageWho actsAdCPAAMP
Buyer agent → sales agentsoftwarecreate_media_buyOrder via OpenDirect, Deals API or OpenRTB
Sales agent → publisherpersonsubmitted: insertion-order reviewOrder or deal approval
Publisher → sales agentpersonApprovedApproved
Sales agent → buyer agentsoftwaremedia_buy_id, committed_metricsOrder or deal ID confirmed

AdCP

  • create_media_buy requires an account, a brand, flight dates, and either packages or a proposal_id with a total budget. po_number and invoice_recipient are optional.
  • The response is completed (synchronous, with a media_buy_id), working (short-running) or submitted (hours or days). Sellers must return submitted when a guaranteed product is ordered and the seller requires insertion-order approval.
  • An optional idempotency_key makes retries safe: a repeated message is not a second order.
  • Confirmed packages carry committed_metrics, described as a binding reporting contract, which are timestamped and immutable.

AAMP

  • announced In the OpenProposal draft, each line item maps to one of three execution standards by how it is transacted: OpenDirect 2.1, the Deals API or OpenRTB 2.6.
  • Agentic Direct provides the communication layer between buyer and seller agents.

Source: AdCP, create_media_buy reference; IAB Tech Lab OpenProposal repository; IAB Tech Lab, framing AAMP · last verified 2026-10-01

Legal lens

High exposure — when exactly is the contract concluded?

With an instant completed response, acceptance is arguably immediate. With submitted, it comes only after human approval. The protocols describe states; they do not define legal acceptance.

Risk sits with: both sides, as uncertainty.
Mitigation: a framework agreement that maps protocol states to legal effect, for example "an order is accepted when a media buy ID or deal ID is issued".

High exposure — agent errors, such as ten times the budget

Rescission for mistake under § 119 BGB is disputed for autonomous systems, because the error lies in the system, not in a human declaration. Relying on rescission is risky.

Risk sits with: the buyer, by default.
Mitigation: a contractual correction window (cancel without cost within a set number of hours for obvious errors); hard caps; plausibility checks on the seller side.

High exposure — whose terms apply?

Agents exchange commercial fields, not legal terms. Without a framework agreement, each side's terms may conflict (the "battle of the forms"), and incorporating terms through an agent message is doubtful.

Risk sits with: both sides.
Mitigation: a master agreement, signed by people, that governs all agent-placed orders.

Medium exposure — duplicate orders

A retried message could double an order. An idempotency key is technical evidence that the second message was a retry, not a new order.

Risk sits with: both sides.
Mitigation: require idempotency keys, and agree that messages with the same key are one order.

Questions to ask the business

  • Which protocol status counts as legal acceptance in our contracts?
  • Is there a cost-free correction window for obvious agent errors?
  • Is a signed framework agreement in place with every seller our agent can reach?
  • Above which value is human approval mandatory on our side too?

Legal sources: § 119 BGB · last verified 2026-10-01

Legal lens

High exposure — intellectual property in AI-generated creatives

Purely AI-generated content may lack copyright protection for want of human authorship, so competitors could reuse it. Outputs may also infringe third-party works or trademarks.

Risk sits with: the advertiser, as the party publishing the ad; the creative-agent provider through an indemnity.
Mitigation: an IP indemnity from the creative-agent vendor; human sign-off on creatives; clearance checks.

High exposure — likeness and personality rights

Synthetic voices or faces resembling real people need consent. A license acquired by an agent must match the actual use: territory, duration and media.

Risk sits with: the advertiser.
Mitigation: check the license scope automatically against flight dates and markets.

Medium exposure — transparency and misleading ads

Art. 50 of the AI Act requires disclosure of deepfakes and other synthetic content, applicable from 2 August 2026; watch for changes through the EU's Digital Omnibus proposals. Unfair-competition law (UWG) prohibits misleading claims and requires ads to be recognizable as ads.

Risk sits with: the advertiser; the publisher for labeling.
Mitigation: map the disclosure and prohibited-claim fields to the legal requirements of each market.

Questions to ask the business

  • Who signs off creatives before they go live — a person, or only the agent?
  • Does our creative-AI vendor give an IP indemnity?
  • Are AI-generated assets labeled where the law requires it?

Legal sources: AI Act (EU) 2024/1689; UWG · last verified 2026-10-01

Legal lens

Medium exposure — changes inside or outside the mandate

Each budget shift is a new contractual declaration. Changes must stay within the original order and the mandate from step 1.

Risk sits with: the buyer.
Mitigation: define in the contract which changes need no new approval.

Medium exposure — third-party code in the seller's platform

Hosting a buyer's agent raises questions of access rights, confidentiality of bid data, IT security and liability for faults the guest code causes.

Risk sits with: the publisher as host and the buyer as code owner.
Mitigation: hosting terms covering sandboxing, audit, data-use limits and fault allocation.

Medium exposure — fraud, brand-safety incidents, outages

Invalid traffic, ads next to harmful content, or runaway spend. Make-goods and refunds are a contract matter.

Risk sits with: whoever the contract names.
Mitigation: a duty to provide a stop switch, incident notification times, make-good rules and invalid-traffic thresholds.

Questions to ask the business

  • Who can stop all agent activity immediately, and how fast?
  • Which in-flight changes need human approval?
  • If we host third-party agent code, on what terms?

Legal lens

Medium exposure — which numbers count?

The seller's measurement and a third party's often differ. Without an agreed source of truth, invoices get disputed.

Risk sits with: both sides.
Mitigation: the contract names the measurement source for each metric and a discrepancy threshold.

Medium exposure — logs as evidence, and how long to keep them

Signed messages and audit logs are strong evidence; German courts assess evidence freely (§ 286 ZPO). Commercial and tax retention duties of six to ten years (§ 257 HGB, § 147 AO) conflict with the GDPR's data minimization where logs contain personal data.

Risk sits with: all parties.
Mitigation: a log retention policy; personal data kept apart from commercial logs; access and audit rights.

Lower exposure — agency transparency

If the agent consolidates billing, the advertiser needs visibility of actual media costs, data fees and rebates.

Risk sits with: the agency.
Mitigation: audit rights and cost-transparency clauses in the agency agreement.

Questions to ask the business

  • What is the contractual source of truth for delivery numbers?
  • How long do we keep agent logs, and do they contain personal data?
  • Do we have audit rights over the agency's and vendors' agent logs?

Legal sources: § 286 ZPO; § 257 HGB; § 147 AO · last verified 2026-10-01

Who carries the risk?

Neither AdCP nor AAMP allocates liability: both are technical specifications. Liability follows the usual rules — contract (framework agreements, terms and conditions, insertion orders), statute (data protection, consumer and competition law, intellectual property, product liability) and attribution (whose agent made the declaration). The practical consequence: people sign the framework, agents fill in the orders. Every party below should have a signed framework agreement before its agents transact.

speculative The default positions below are indicative, based on an EU and German law reading. The contracts in place decide the actual allocation.

    The principal: its money, its brand.

    Primarily answers for

    • Framework agreements before agents transact
    • Mandatory human review for regulated categories
    • IP and data indemnities from vendors

  • Agency (operator)

    Configures and runs the buyer agent.

    Primarily answers for

    • Configuring limits and the governance mode correctly
    • Payment, where it is the contracting party under the operator billing model
    • Transparency to the advertiser on costs and rebates

    How to protect yourself

    • A clear split of configuration responsibility with the advertiser
    • Logged change control
    • A liability cap in the agency agreement
    • Vendor service levels passed through

  • Agent technology provider

    Builds the buyer, sales or creative agent software.

    Primarily answers for

    Primarily answers for

Glossary

Agent
Software, usually built on a large language model, that takes actions by itself within limits its operator sets.
Buyer agent, sales agent
The agent acting for the advertiser, and the one acting for the publisher.
Operator
The company that runs and configures an agent, often the agency. AdCP models it explicitly.
MCP, A2A
Model Context Protocol and Agent2Agent protocol: the transports agents use to talk to each other.
Insertion order
The traditional signed order document for a media buy. Agents create its digital equivalent.
Deal ID
The identifier of a pre-negotiated private deal that is executed through programmatic auctions (OpenRTB).
Idempotency key
A unique identifier per order that stops a retried message from creating a duplicate order.
RFC 9421 signature
Cryptographic signing of HTTP messages, which proves who sent a message and that it was not altered.
